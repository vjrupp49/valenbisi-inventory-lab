# Simulation findings (Phase 7)

Updated 2026-11-12 on the frozen dataset `data-freeze-2026-11-11` (the first version, on 15 days, is in [archive/freeze-2026-10-24/](archive/freeze-2026-10-24/)).
Numbers: [simulation_results.md](simulation_results.md) (check IDs like **E3b** point to tables there), generated by
`src/simulate/run_empirical.py`. Method details: `src/simulate/empirical.py`.

**This is a "what if" machine, not a measurement.** It compares rebalancing strategies on a replica of the real network
under stated assumptions. It cannot tell us what Valenbisi would really save. Its value is in comparing strategies with
each other and showing which assumptions matter.

## What the simulation is
A replica of the real network: 272 real stations with their real capacities, starting from their real overnight bike
levels, simulated for 5 days at a time, 100 times over. Riders arrive randomly; when a station is empty they cannot rent
(a **lost pickup**); when it is full a returning bike circulates and tries again an hour later. Five
strategies are compared:
- **none:** no rebalancing;
- **static:** a fixed set of 20 stations is visited;
- **reactive:** at each round, move bikes from the fullest to the emptiest stations using current counts;
- **forecast-driven:** same, but plan using a forecast of the next 1, 3 or 6 hours, with forecast quality set to match what the real model achieved;
- **oracle:** a perfect-knowledge forecast (3 hours), as an upper bound on what better forecasting could do.

All policies get the same budget (6 transfers per round, 4 rounds a day, 24 a day) and the same simulated riders.

## What comes from the data and what is assumed
| From the frozen data | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Station list and capacities, overnight starting levels | Rebalancing budget, round times (06, 10, 14, 18) and target fill (half full) |
| Pickup and return patterns per station and hour of day (counts only; moves of 8+ bikes treated as truck moves and left out) | Demand scale (counted pickups are a lower bound on true trips), tested at 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 |
| Day-to-day variation (city-wide 0.22, station-level 0.18) and bursty arrivals (0.29) | Which 20 stations the static route visits |
| Average trip time of about 19 minutes (0.318 h), from how far station levels dip during the day (Little's law) | Bikes that find a full station simply try again an hour later |
| Forecast quality, matched to the measured accuracy of the real model (AUC 0.81 at 3 hours, 0.71 at 6 hours) | Riders at an empty station give up (no detour to a neighbour) |

Context for the budget: the data shows about 27 jumps of 8 or more bikes up and 28 down per day (E1). If those are truck
moves (a hypothesis; there is no truck data), a budget of 24 transfers a day is the same size as real activity.

## Is the replica realistic? (E2b)
- **Fair:** hourly patterns match the real ones (correlation 0.88 for empty stations, about 0.93 for full ones). Stations rank the same way: the simulated empty, full and average levels correlate with the real ones at 0.84, 0.86 and 0.90 across stations. Full stations match well (6.0% simulated versus 6.3% real). Average bikes per station is close (7.4 versus 7.0).
- **Not fair:** the simulation understates empty stations, at 7.7% of station-time against 12.8% in reality, including at every time of day. Missing ingredients include sub-hour clustering, geography, breakdowns, and how riders really move between nearby stations.
- **So:** do not read the absolute numbers of lost pickups (around 2,600 a day with no rebalancing) as real. They are inflated by stations that are empty most of the time. Treat only the comparisons between strategies as informative, and even those carry the assumptions above.
- Two fixes were needed to get here and are worth knowing: a first version, with returns independent of pickups, let the fleet drift (bikes appeared from nowhere); and an early fix that sent displaced bikes to the emptiest stations acted as free rebalancing, which would have flattered every policy. Both were replaced.

## Results (baseline, 100 replications; E3, E3b)
- **Rebalancing helps, a bit:** reactive rebalancing avoids about 93 lost pickups a day out of about 2,605 (3.6%) (CI 86 to 100).
- **Smart planning beats a fixed route:** the static route avoids only about 23 a day, and the reactive policy beats it by 70 a day (CI 63 to 78).
- **Forecasting adds a small amount on top of reactive:** planning with a 3-hour forecast at the measured accuracy beats reactive by about 33 lost pickups a day (CI 26 to 39), roughly 1.3% fewer. A 1-hour forecast adds 16.9 (CI 12.3 to 21.5). A perfect 3-hour forecast adds 45.1 (CI 38.9 to 51.4), so the measured-accuracy forecast captures about 73% of what perfect foresight could add under this budget.
- **A 6-hour forecast hurts:** it does worse than reactive by about 56 lost pickups a day (CI 48 to 63), because projections that far ahead are too noisy to plan on. This echoes the forecast results, where the model's edge at 6 hours disappeared.
- The 1-hour forecast could not be matched exactly: the real model's AUC (0.93) is slightly higher than the simulated forecast reaches even with no noise (0.91), so it was run noise-free (E2).

## What matters more: forecast quality or budget? (E4)
- **The budget matters most.** At the baseline demand, reactive rebalancing avoids about 57 lost pickups a day with 3 transfers per round, 96 with 6, and 152 with 12.
- **Forecasting helps more when there is more capacity to move bikes.** The 3-hour forecast beats reactive by about 11 a day at 3 transfers per round (CI 4 to 18), 36 at 6 (CI 26 to 46), and 77 at 12 (CI 67 to 88). With 12 transfers it captures about 80% of the perfect-forecast gain (96 a day).
- **Higher assumed demand makes the absolute numbers bigger and the forecast edge larger at small budgets:** with demand 1.5 times what we counted, the forecast edge is 31 a day at 3 transfers (CI 21 to 41) and 97 at 12 (CI 74 to 120); at twice, 28 (CI 11 to 45) and 99 (CI 75 to 124).
- **Implication:** even a good forecast needs enough trucks and a good planning rule to pay off. Doubling the budget from 6 to 12 transfers per round avoids about 56 more lost pickups a day under reactive rebalancing, more than the forecast adds at 6 transfers (36 a day). This model says improving forecasts alone is not the bottleneck, though the forecast is worth more the more trucks there are.

## What this does and does not show
- It **supports** a modest, assumption-bound argument: forecast-driven planning with 1 to 3 hours of lead time should beat purely reactive rebalancing, by a small amount at today's budget and more with a larger budget.
- It **does not show** real savings, the right number of trucks, or that Valenbisi's real operations are anything like the "reactive" policy.
- The conclusion about forecasts rests on translating a forecast score (AUC) into simulated noise, which is a rough matching of ranking skill only.

## What changed with more data (15 days to 33 days)
- **Stable:** the ordering of strategies. Reactive beats a fixed route; the 1-hour and 3-hour forecasts beat reactive; the 6-hour forecast loses to reactive; the budget matters more than forecast quality.
- **Not stable:** the size of the effects. Reactive avoided 127 lost pickups a day with 15 days of inputs and 93 with 33; the 3-hour forecast edge over reactive was 21 a day and is now 33. The estimated city-wide day-to-day variation fell from 0.29 to 0.22. Treat the sizes as uncertain to within tens of pickups a day, and the ordering as the finding.

## Limits
- One 33-day window in one autumn month; demand rates are estimated, not known, and counted pickups are a lower bound.
- No geography (riders cannot detour, trucks have no travel times), no breakdowns, no weather.
- The simulation understates how often stations are empty (see above).
- Planning is a simple greedy rule; a better optimiser might change how much a forecast is worth.

## Next
- Re-run when new freezes (60 and 90 days) are done, since the demand estimates and the forecast accuracy will both change.
- Possible improvements, only if worthwhile: nearby-station detours using station coordinates, and a better planning rule.
